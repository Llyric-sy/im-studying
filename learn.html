ACCT2002 · 20–26 Jul · Week 1

Lesson 1 · The Manager & Management Accounting

The point of management accounting is to help people inside a business make better decisions. It is not mainly about producing the external financial statements.

business events + data→management accountant→useful info→manager decides→results reviewed

Management vs financial accounting

Management accounting is internal, decision-focused, can be future-oriented and can be produced whenever managers need it. Financial accounting mainly serves external users, is more historical/aggregated and follows reporting rules.

Three roles

Scorekeeping = report what happened. Attention directing = flag something needing attention. Problem solving = analyse alternatives and help decide what to do next.

Decision cycle

1 identify problem/uncertainty · 2 obtain information · 3 predict future outcomes · 4 choose among alternatives · 5 implement, evaluate, learn.

Planning, control, ethics

Planning selects goals/strategies; a budget quantifies a proposed plan. Control compares results with the plan and uses feedback. Ethics themes include competence, confidentiality, integrity and credibility.

Applied recognition

  • Preparing last year’s sales report → scorekeeping.
  • Highlighting which product line declined → attention directing.
  • Comparing alternative warranty strategies → problem solving.
Watch for this: budgeting is not the whole of planning; it is one quantified part of a broader planning process.

Before moving on

  • Explain management vs financial accounting in your own words.
  • Classify record / flag / solve examples.
  • Recite the five decision steps in order.

Lesson 2 · Strategy

Strategy is the organisation deciding where it wants to go and how it intends to win over the long run. Management accounting supports that by supplying information about customers, competitors, costs and resources.

vision / mission / objectives→analyse outside + inside→where to compete→how to compete

Vision, mission, objectives

Vision = desired future state. Mission = purpose and boundaries. Objectives = specific aims, often measurable/time-related.

SWOT + Five Forces

SWOT: strengths/weaknesses are internal; opportunities/threats are external. Porter: buyer power, supplier power, substitutes, new entrants, rivalry.

Levels of strategy

Corporate = what businesses? Business = how compete? Functional = what systems/activities support that strategy?

Competitive advantage

Cost leadership wins through lower cost/productivity/scale. Differentiation wins through something customers perceive as meaningfully unique.

Case clue method

  • Low price, standard features, productivity, tight cost control → usually cost leadership.
  • Unique features, quality, craftsmanship, premium service → usually differentiation.
  • Answer pattern: name framework → cite case fact → explain strategic effect.
Watch for this: profitability is not automatically competitive advantage; the comparison is against competitors.

Lesson 3 · Cost Terms & Purposes

Before calculating anything, ask: what is the cost object, what decision is the cost for, and are we talking total or per-unit cost?

cost occurs→choose cost object→trace or allocate→classify behaviour→RM → WIP → FG → COGS

Direct vs indirect

Direct costs can be conveniently/economically traced to the chosen cost object. Indirect costs cannot, so they are allocated. The classification depends on the cost object.

Variable vs fixed

Variable: total changes with activity, per-unit stays constant. Fixed: total stays constant within the relevant range, per-unit changes as volume changes.

Manufacturing categories

DM + DL + MOH = product/inventoriable cost for a manufacturer. Selling/admin are normally period costs.

Flow formulas

DM used = Op RM + Purchases − End RM
TMC = DM + DL + MOH
COGM = Op WIP + TMC − End WIP
COGS = Op FG + COGM − End FG

Why the three inventories matter

  • RM adjusts purchases to get material actually used.
  • WIP adjusts manufacturing cost for unfinished production to get COGM.
  • FG adjusts COGM for what was actually sold to get COGS.
Watch for this: “direct/indirect” and “product/period” are different classifications. Advertising can be directly linked to a campaign but is still not manufacturing product cost.

Lesson 4 · Determining How Costs Behave

You are separating a mixed cost into the part that exists regardless of activity and the part that rises with activity.

activity + cost observations→choose causal driver→estimate a + b→predict with y = a + bX

The equation

y = a + bX. y = predicted cost; a = fixed intercept; b = variable cost per unit/slope; X = cost driver.

High-low

Use the highest and lowest activity observations. b = change in cost ÷ change in activity; then solve for a with one point.

Regression

Regression uses all observations. Evaluate economic plausibility, goodness of fit and significance of the independent variable instead of blindly accepting the output.

IQ-2 utilities

  • High: 61,500 hours at $36,150; low: 31,500 at $33,150.
  • b = (36,150−33,150)/(61,500−31,500) = $0.10/hour.
  • a = 36,150 − 61,500×0.10 = $30,000.
  • Equation: Utilities = 30,000 + 0.10X. At 39,000 hours → $33,900.
Watch for this: high-low uses highest/lowest cost-driver activity, not highest/lowest cost.

Lesson 5 · Job Costing

Job costing fits distinct jobs that consume different resources. Direct costs are traced; manufacturing overhead is allocated using a chosen base and rate.

identify job→trace DM + DL→choose OH base→calculate predetermined OH rate→apply OH

Job vs process

Job costing suits distinct jobs/products. Process costing suits large numbers of identical/similar units where averaging makes sense.

Normal costing

A predetermined overhead rate uses budgeted overhead ÷ budgeted allocation-base quantity. Applied OH = predetermined rate × actual allocation base used by the job.

Year-end adjustment

If allocated OH differs from actual OH, overhead is underallocated or overallocated and must be adjusted/disposed of.

Core logic

  • If budgeted MOH = $600,000 and budgeted machine hours = 30,000, rate = $20/MH.
  • A job using 240 MH receives $4,800 allocated MOH.
  • Then total job cost combines DM + DL + applied OH.
Watch for this: normal costing applies overhead using a predetermined rate; it does not wait for actual full-year overhead before costing each job.

Lesson 6 · Process Costing

Process costing spreads cost across many similar units. The tricky part is unfinished units, so we convert partial work into equivalent completed units.

physical units→equivalent units→costs to account for→cost per equivalent unit→assign to completed + ending WIP

Equivalent units

Equivalent units convert partial work into the amount of fully completed output that work represents. Two half-complete units = one equivalent completed unit.

Five steps

1 physical flow · 2 equivalent units · 3 total costs to account for · 4 cost/equivalent unit · 5 assign costs to transferred-out units and ending WIP.

Weighted average

Weighted-average blends beginning WIP work/cost with current-period work/cost rather than separating periods.

Quick equivalent-units example

  • 10,000 ending-WIP units at 70% conversion = 7,000 equivalent units of conversion work.
  • Materials and conversion can have different completion percentages, so calculate equivalent units separately.
Watch for this: in step 2 focus on quantities first. Do not mix dollar amounts into equivalent-unit calculations prematurely.

ACCT2002 · 7–13 Sep · Week 8

Lesson 7 · Activity-Based Costing & Management

ABC exists because one broad overhead rate can make simple/high-volume products subsidise complex/low-volume products. ABC tries to assign overhead according to the activities that actually cause it.

resources→activity cost pools→cost drivers→products / services

Undercosting / overcosting

Undercosting = a product consumes lots of resources but receives too little cost. Overcosting = consumes fewer resources but gets too much cost. Together they can create cross-subsidisation.

Four-level hierarchy

1 output unit-level · 2 batch-level · 3 product/service-sustaining · 4 facility-sustaining.

ABC trade-off

More refined drivers can improve accuracy, but ABC requires more measurement, maintenance and judgement. A needlessly detailed system can cost more than its benefit.

Why a single rate can mislead

  • Product A: huge volume, few setups/inspections.
  • Product B: low volume, many setups/inspections.
  • A single machine-hour rate may dump too much OH on A. ABC allocates setup/inspection pools based on the drivers B actually uses.
Watch for this: a convenient driver is not automatically a good driver. It should have a sensible cause-and-effect relationship with the activity cost.

Lesson 8 · Pricing Decisions & Cost Management

Price comes from the interaction of customers, competitors and costs. The firm cannot just look inward at cost and ignore the market.

customers+competitors+costs→pricing approach→target return / target cost

Market vs cost based

Market-based asks what price customers/competitors allow. Cost-based/cost-plus starts with cost and adds the required return.

Target costing

Target cost = target selling price − target profit. It is price-led costing: the market price comes first, then the firm works backward to allowable cost.

Life-cycle / locked-in costs

Design choices can lock in future costs before the cash is actually spent. Managing cost early in R&D/design can therefore matter more than late-stage cutting.

AQ-14 Leno

  • Total unit cost = 30 + 75 + 50 + 25 = $180.
  • 25% markup → current cost-plus price = $225.
  • Comparable market price = $195, so simply insisting on $225 is not realistic if customers have equivalent alternatives.
Watch for this: cost-plus runs cost → markup → price; target costing runs market price → target profit → allowable cost.

Lesson 9 · Master Budget & Responsibility Accounting

A master budget links operational assumptions into one coordinated plan. Sales drives production; production drives materials/labour/overhead; those feed cash and financial statements.

sales budget→production→DM + DL + MOH→COGS + operating costs→income + cash + balance sheet

Budget logic

A budget is a quantitative expression of a proposed plan for a specified period. It provides a road map and a benchmark for later control.

Production formula

Required production = budgeted sales + desired ending FG − beginning FG.

Responsibility accounting

Responsibility accounting links information/performance to the area a manager controls. Budgets also affect behaviour and incentives, not just numbers.

AQ-15 Wesley

  • Sales budget starts with units × $44.
  • Desired ending FG = 30% of next month’s sales, so production must include this inventory target.
  • Each unit uses 0.75 labour hours × $18 = $13.50 direct labour per produced unit.
Watch for this: do not build each budget in isolation. The master budget is a linked system.

ECOM Lesson 1 · Business Analytics

Business analytics means using data, quantitative methods and models to support a business decision — not doing Excel for its own sake.

business question→data→analyse→interpret→decision

Three questions

Descriptive: what happened? Predictive: what is likely to happen? Prescriptive: what should we do?

Study habit

Before using a formula, write down the business question and what the output would mean for a decision.

ECOM Lesson 2 · Tables, Sorting & Filtering

Good analysis starts with data arranged consistently. One row should represent one record; columns represent fields/attributes.

Sort vs filter

Sorting changes order. Filtering temporarily shows only records that meet conditions. Neither changes the underlying meaning of the data.

Excel trap

A plain SUM can still total hidden/filtered rows. For criterion-based answers, use the appropriate conditional function such as SUMIF/SUMIFS when that is what the task requires.

ECOM Lesson 3 · Database Analytics

Use logical, database and lookup functions to answer questions from large datasets consistently instead of manually scanning rows.

IF / AND / OR

These convert business rules into logical tests. First say the rule in words, then write the formula.

Lookups

Identify the key you are matching and the field you want returned. VLOOKUP or INDEX/MATCH can then retrieve the corresponding value.

Watch for this: plausible-looking output can still be wrong if the criteria or lookup range is wrong.

ECOM Lesson 4 · Descriptive Statistics

Describe where values sit, how often they occur and what a typical value looks like.

Centre

Mean uses all values; median is the middle ordered value; mode is the most frequent. Skew/outliers can make the median more representative than the mean.

Position

Percentiles and quartiles describe relative position in ordered data; histograms/frequency distributions show the overall shape.

ECOM Lesson 5 · Measures of Association

Ask whether two variables move together and how strong that relationship appears.

Covariance / correlation

Covariance indicates direction of joint movement. Correlation standardises association so direction/strength are easier to compare.

Outliers

Outliers can materially change the measured relationship, so investigate them rather than automatically deleting them.

Watch for this: correlation is association, not proof of causation.

ECOM Lesson 6 · Data Visualisation

Choose a visual because it answers the question clearly, not because it looks impressive.

Chart choice

Trend over time → line/time-series style. Relationship between two numeric variables → scatter style. Category comparison → bar/column style often works well.

Communication

Titles, axes, units and scale should let someone interpret the chart without guessing.

ECOM Lesson 7 · PivotTables

PivotTables group and summarise large datasets without rewriting the underlying source table.

Fields

Decide what defines rows, columns and filters, then choose the numeric value and aggregation such as sum, count or average.

Interpretation

Always be able to explain exactly what one PivotTable cell represents in business terms.

Watch for this: Excel may automatically choose count instead of sum. Check the aggregation.

ECOM Lesson 8 · Forecasting & Regression

Use patterns in historical data to estimate relationships and future values, then judge whether the model is credible enough to use.

Regression

Identify dependent vs independent variables and interpret coefficients in the units/context of the problem.

Forecasting

Time-series data are ordered through time; trend and other time structure matter. Avoid treating extrapolation far beyond observed data as automatically reliable.

ECOM Lesson 9 · Statistical Inference

Use sample evidence to make a reasoned statement about a wider population while explicitly dealing with uncertainty.

Population vs sample

The population is the full group of interest. The sample is the subset observed. A sample statistic helps infer a population parameter.

Uncertainty

Different samples can produce different results, so inference cannot pretend the sample result is exact for the entire population.

ECOM Lesson 10 · Data Governance & Ethics

Ask whether data are controlled, used and communicated responsibly — not merely whether the analysis is technically correct.

collect→store / access→analyse→share / report→retain / dispose

Governance

Clear ownership, access controls, rules and processes around data.

Ethics

Identify stakeholders, possible harm, fairness, misleading use and whether the purpose can be justified.

Watch for this: “technically allowed” does not automatically answer the ethical question.
